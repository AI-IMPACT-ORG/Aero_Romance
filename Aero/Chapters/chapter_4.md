# Morgen

The spring winds brought more than just tourists to Ærø. Katrine had been at the clinic for a week when the first case of Baltic Fever arrived. The patient, an elderly fisherman named Erik, was carried in by his grandson, his joints swollen and his skin unusually sensitive to the clinic's fluorescent lights. The scent of the sea clung to his clothes, mingling with the sharp, clean smell of the clinic's antiseptic.

"Det er tidligt i år," Mads muttered, checking Erik's pulse. "The spring winds are stronger than usual." His fingers moved with practiced precision, but Katrine noticed the concern in his eyes. "His temperature is elevated, and the joint inflammation is more severe than last year's cases."

Katrine reached for her grandmother's journal, which she'd been studying every night. The symptoms matched perfectly - the joint pain, the light sensitivity, the timing with the spring winds. But seeing it in person was different from reading about it in the journal. The way Erik's hands trembled, the way he winced at the light - these were details no textbook could capture.

"Let me try something," she said, heading to the herb garden. The elderberry and sea buckthorn were just beginning to bud, but she'd prepared a batch of the remedy the night before, following her grandmother's instructions precisely. The storm's breath plants glowed softly in their greenhouse, their energy seeming to pulse with the rhythm of the wind.

Mads watched with interest as she administered the traditional remedy. "Your grandmother's formula?" he asked, his voice low and warm.

Katrine nodded, her medical training warring with the island's wisdom. "With a few modern modifications. I've been studying the active compounds." She added a precise measurement of the storm's breath extract, watching as it reacted with the other ingredients, creating a subtle luminescence in the mixture.

The change in Erik was almost immediate. His breathing eased, and the tension in his face relaxed. "Dr. Marie's touch," he said, his voice rough but clear. "I'd know it anywhere." His weathered hand reached out, patting Katrine's arm. "You have her hands, child. The same healing touch."

Mads's eyes met Katrine's over the patient's bed. There was something new in his gaze - respect, yes, but something more. A recognition that went beyond professional admiration. The way he looked at her made her heart skip a beat, and she found herself remembering the moment in the greenhouse, when their hands had almost touched.

The rest of the day was a blur of patients and preparations. More cases of Baltic Fever arrived, each one confirming what her grandmother had documented. Katrine found herself falling into a rhythm, combining modern diagnostics with traditional treatments, while Mads handled the more routine cases. Their movements around each other became a dance, each anticipating the other's needs, each complementing the other's skills.

By evening, the clinic was quiet. The last patient had been treated, and the herb garden was bathed in golden light. Katrine was in the garden, checking the elderberry bushes, when Mads joined her. The setting sun caught in his hair, turning it to copper, and she found herself watching the way the light played across his face.

"You've been studying the Baltic Fever," he said, not a question but an observation. His voice was soft, carrying the musical lilt of the island's dialect.

"Since I found my grandmother's journal," she replied. "There's something about it that doesn't fit any modern diagnosis." She reached out, touching a sea buckthorn leaf, her fingers brushing against his as he did the same.

Mads nodded, his hand lingering near hers. "I've been trying to understand it for years. The symptoms are consistent, but the cause..." He trailed off, looking at the sea buckthorn bushes. "Your grandmother had a theory."

Katrine's heart skipped. "What theory?" The wind caught her hair, and Mads reached out, tucking it behind her ear. The gesture was becoming familiar, but it still sent a shiver through her.

"That it's not a disease at all," he said, his voice low and intimate in the quiet garden. "That it's the island's way of adapting to the changing seasons. The symptoms are the body's response to the unique combination of sea air and spring winds."

"That's why traditional remedies work better than modern medicine," Katrine realized, her medical mind making connections even as her heart responded to his nearness. "They're not fighting the condition - they're helping the body adapt."

"Exactly." Mads's voice was warm with approval. "Your grandmother was ahead of her time. She understood that sometimes the old ways work better because they've evolved with the environment."

They worked in comfortable silence for a while, tending to the herb garden. The Baltic wind carried the scent of the sea, and Katrine found herself watching Mads's hands as he carefully pruned the sea buckthorn. There was something about the way he moved, the way he treated each plant with respect, that reminded her of her grandmother.

"I've been thinking," he said finally, his voice breaking the comfortable silence. "We should document this properly. Your grandmother's methods, the modern adaptations, the whole approach. It could help other island communities."

Katrine felt a surge of excitement. This was exactly what she'd been looking for - a way to bridge the gap between traditional and modern medicine, to honor her grandmother's legacy while advancing medical understanding. But it was more than that. It was a chance to build something with Mads, to combine their skills and knowledge in a way that could help others.

"I'd like that," she said. "Very much."

Mads smiled, and in the golden light of the setting sun, Katrine saw something in his eyes that made her heart race. It wasn't just professional respect anymore. It was something deeper, something that had been growing since the moment he'd caught her grandmother's stethoscope.

The Baltic wind picked up, carrying the scent of herbs and sea salt. Katrine took a deep breath, feeling the weight of her decision settle on her shoulders. This was her path now - not just as a doctor, but as a bridge between two worlds. And somehow, Mads had become part of that journey.

"I should check on Erik," she said, suddenly aware of how close they were standing.

Mads nodded, but his eyes stayed on hers for a moment longer. "I'll finish up here," he said. "Meet you inside?"

Katrine nodded, her heart still racing as she turned toward the clinic. The mystery of the Baltic Fever, the connection to her grandmother's legacy, the growing bond with Mads - it all felt like pieces of a puzzle falling into place.

The spring winds might have brought the Baltic Fever, but they'd also brought something else. Something that felt like the beginning of a new chapter in her life.

And this time, she was ready to write it with him.
